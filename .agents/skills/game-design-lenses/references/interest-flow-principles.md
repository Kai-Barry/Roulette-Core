# Interest Curve, Flow & Player Motivation Principles

Distilled from *The Art of Game Design* (Schell, 3rd ed.), Chapters 9 and 14.

## The Interest Curve (Chapter 14)

Experiences can be judged by how interest changes over time. Plot **interest level** on the vertical axis against **time** on the horizontal axis. The shape matters more than absolute values — only relative changes count.

**Anatomy of a good interest curve:**

1. **A hook** — an initial spike of interest that grabs the player immediately (first contact within seconds).
2. **Gradually rising interest**, punctuated by **periods of rest** — relentless escalation exhausts players; valleys let interest rebuild (tension → release → bigger tension).
3. **A grand finale** — the most interesting moment comes near the end; never let the climax fizzle.
4. **Fractal structure** — good interest curves exist at every scale: the whole session, a level, a mission, a 30-second skirmish — each with hook, rises, rests, finale.

**Interest = inherent interest + proximal interest (value of future events).** A flat or descending curve kills the experience. To judge, sketch the curve for each player archetype (Lens #16 × Lens #61) and compare intuitions with playtesters' own sketches.

## Inherent Interest

Some things are simply more interesting than others:

- Risk is more interesting than safety; fancy more than plain; the unusual more than the ordinary.
- Base instincts (survival, food, danger, status) grab attention immediately.
- Higher instincts (love, honor, justice, curiosity, art) hold it longer and create meaning.
- Great games appeal to as many levels of interest as possible.

**Beauty** (Lens #63): beautiful things are inherently interesting; ugliness in service of the experience is still craft, but unintentional ugliness reads as carelessness.

## Flow (Chapter 9)

Flow is the state of complete, energized focus in an activity — the feeling you want players to have. Requirements:

- **Clear goals** — the player knows what to do.
- **No distractions** — nothing steals focus from the task.
- **Direct feedback** — every action's effect is immediately visible.
- **Continuously challenging** — challenge stays in the margin between boredom and anxiety as skill grows.

The **flow channel** is a dynamic path: as skill rises, challenge must rise with it; when players grow bored, add challenge; when anxious, let them build skill (or reduce friction). Boredom and anxiety are both exits from flow — both must be managed.

Flow is hard to test: it won't show in 10 minutes; observe longer sessions. Solo players in flow go quiet; multiplayer players chatter constantly about the game.

## Motivation & Needs (Lens #19)

Games operate on **Maslow's hierarchy** — most games operate on the higher levels (belonging, esteem, actualization) but must not neglect lower ones (comfort, safety). Players cannot enjoy esteem if the interface makes them feel unsafe or confused. A game can't merely promise a need — it must deliver fulfillment of the need.

## Judgment (Lens #20)

Games are structures of judgment — they judge players constantly (scores, win/lose, comparisons). Players **want** to be judged, but want it to be **fair**, they must **care** about the judgment, and it should make them **want to improve**. An opaque, unfair, or pointless judgment destroys motivation.

## Curiosity & the Interest Gap (Lenses #4, #5)

Motivation = the gap between what the player knows and wants to know, powered by **endogenous value**: the goal must be worth something *inside* the game's value system. If in-game rewards don't matter in-game, curiosity stalls.

## Key Audit Questions for Interest & Flow

- Sketch the interest curve of the whole game and of each level/loop: is there a hook, rising interest with rest, and a grand finale?
- Are there dead spots (flat/declining interest) anywhere? What would fill them?
- Does challenge track skill (flow channel), including for skilled veterans?
- Is feedback immediate enough to sustain flow, and are distractions (loading, menus, unclear UI) minimized?
- Which base and higher instincts does the experience appeal to — and are more available?
- Does the judgment system communicate fairly, and does the player care about it?
