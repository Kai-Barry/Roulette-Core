# Story, Indirect Control & World Principles

Distilled from *The Art of Game Design* (Schell, 3rd ed.), Chapters 15–18.

## Story vs. Gameplay (Chapter 15)

Story and gameplay are the two great forces of game design. The dream: **the story machine** (Lens #65) — a game whose events *are* the story, generated by play rather than told. Weave story into the tetrad: story should support gameplay, aesthetics, and technology — and be supported by them in return.

**Story tips for game designers:**

1. **Story events must be caused by gameplay, not bypass it.** If story is told in cutscenes while gameplay is unrelated, the game is a movie with an intermission. Make the conflict of the story be the conflict of gameplay.
2. **Stories give purpose to gameplay** — the why behind the mechanics.
3. **Story structures**: the Hero's Journey (ordinary world → call → refusal → mentor → threshold → trials → ordeal → reward → return) is a *form*, not a formula — use as a skeleton, not a checklist.
4. **The obstacle** (Lens #66): obstacles are the atoms of both story and gameplay; the same design should serve both.
5. **Simplicity and transcendence** (Lens #67): simple, focused worlds with a transcendent twist are memorable (Portal, Tetris).
6. **The weirdest thing** (Lens #69): identify the weirdest element of your story — make sure it doesn't confuse or alienate, and if there are several weird things, coalesce them.
7. **Use clichés judiciously** — clichés are a shared language; use them to set expectations cheaply, then subvert one for surprise.
8. **Freedom** (Lens #71): the more freedom story gives, the harder it is to tell — decide consciously how much freedom vs. authored narrative your design needs.

## Indirect Control (Chapter 16)

The problem: players want freedom, but the design needs them in particular places doing particular things. **Direct control** (cutscenes, forced movement) is easy but unsatisfying. **Indirect control** shapes player desire so players *want* to do what the designer needs.

**Six methods of indirect control:**

1. **Constraints** — remove all other options; a narrow path feels free when it's the only visible one.
2. **Goals** — players go where their goals are; set goals that lead through your intended content.
3. **Interface** — the interface can make the right action easy and the wrong one hard (a door that only opens inward).
4. **Visual design** — light the intended path; use lines, silhouettes, and composition to pull the eye where you want it.
5. **Characters** — in-game characters beckon, lead, and model behavior (a friendly NPC waving from the bridge).
6. **Music** — sound steers mood and attention (ominous music near danger, cheerful near safety).

Also **collusion** (Lens #73): in multiplayer, designers can nudge players into directing each other (traitor mechanics, escort missions, social contracts like a caller in puzzle games).

## Worlds & Characters (Chapters 17–18)

A **world** (Lens #74) is more than a level: it has geography, architecture, culture, and inner consistency. **The nameless quality** (Lens #83, after Alexander's "quality without a name"): worlds feel alive when they have "life" — wholeness, freedom, exactness, community — each part fitting the whole.

**Characters:**

- **Avatar** (Lens #75): the player's face in the world; long-nose (never seen) or long-suit (a full character).
- **Character function** (Lens #76): every character should do something — protagonist, antagonist, ally, rival, mentor.
- **Character traits** (Lens #77): show traits in what characters say and do, not in what they claim.
- **Interpersonal circumplex** (Lens #78): model relationships on two axes — friendliness (hostile ↔ friendly) and dominance (submissive ↔ dominant). Every relationship has a position; movement on the map creates drama.
- **Character web** (Lens #79): map all characters' relationships; a healthy cast is a web, not a star with spokes.
- **Status** (Lens #80): characters signal status constantly (high: eye contact, space; low: avoidance, smallness); status contests are drama engines.
- **Character transformation** (Lens #81): the most compelling characters change over the story; the world should enable that change.
- **Inner contradiction** (Lens #82): a character who wants two incompatible things is interesting.

**Projection** (Lens #64): players fall in love with characters they can project onto — give them things to relate to, imagine, visit, be, meet, do, and keep doing.

## Key Audit Questions for Story & Control

- Does gameplay cause the story (or vice versa), or do they merely alternate?
- Could a player describe what the story is *about* through play alone?
- Where does the design need players to go — which indirect-control method fits best, and does the player retain the *feeling* of freedom?
- Is the world internally consistent, and does every location have a reason to exist?
- Does each character earn their place (function), show their traits through action, and sit somewhere meaningful on the circumplex?
- What is the weirdest thing in this story, and is it doing work rather than confusion?
